### Exercise 1.1 — Self-Play

- **Book location:** Section 1.5, PDF page 28.
- **Type:** Theory.
- **Problem:** The tic-tac-toe player could look ahead via a model of its environment (or play
  against a random opponent). Suppose the reinforcement learning algorithm played against itself,
  with both sides learning. What would happen? Would it learn a different policy for selecting moves?
**Hook.** Let's remove the fixed opponent entirely and let the learner play against a copy of
itself. The question is whether two moving targets can settle on a sensible strategy, or whether
they just chase each other forever.

**Formal bridge.** In a zero-sum game, the value of a state under optimal play satisfies the
minimax backup

\[
q_*(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[r + \gamma \max_{a'} q_*(s',a') \mid s,a\right],
\tag{1.1.1}
\]

and self-play replaces the fixed-opponent expectation by data generated by both learners'
current policies \(\pi^1_k\) and \(\pi^2_k\):

\[
(s,a,r,s') \sim p(\cdot \mid s,a), \quad a \sim \pi^1_k(\cdot \mid s),\quad a' \sim \pi^2_k(\cdot \mid s').
\tag{1.1.2}
\]

where \(s\) is the current board, \(a\) and \(a'\) are moves by the two sides, \(r\) is the
terminal reward, \(s'\) is the next board, \(\gamma\) is the discount factor, and \(\pi^1_k,
\pi^2_k\) are the policies of the two learning players at iteration \(k\).

**Reasoning chain.** The key structural fact is that tic-tac-toe gives the two players identical
move sets and mirror-image rewards: one side's win is the other's loss. Because of that symmetry,
nothing in (1.1.1) favours either role. If both learners see the same states and use the same
learning rule, their estimates track the same target, so the policies they approach are the same
optimal strategy applied from opposite sides of the board — mirror-image policies.

What do those policies look like? With perfect play on a board where both sides can force at
least a draw, neither player can win, so the terminal outcome is a draw and the converged value is
the draw value for every reachable state. The book's learner already has this flavour: its
lookahead search evaluates moves by the best replies it can find. Self-play supplies exactly
those best replies as training data, so both sides are pushed toward optimal play rather than
toward exploiting a weak opponent.

The practical caveat is that the target in (1.1.2) moves while both players learn. Early on,
each side's exploration changes the distribution of boards the other sees, so the value being
estimated is not stationary: estimates can fluctuate, converge slowly, or temporarily diverge
before the policies stabilise. Once exploration settles and both policies are near optimal, the
feedback loop becomes stable, and the mirror-image structure reappears.

**Why it makes sense.** Think of the extreme where one player is already perfect and the other
is still random. The perfect player sees an easy, biased stream of boards and can look greedy;
the random player sees a brutal opponent and cannot learn much except "most moves lose". Under
self-play neither side gets that fixed easy target — the opponent keeps improving along with
you — so the only stable outcome is the game-theoretic one: neither can win against itself.
The direction is toward drawing play, not toward one side dominating, precisely because the
roles are interchangeable.

**Check your understanding.** Prediction: start the two players from identical value tables and
identical exploration rates, and symmetry is preserved throughout — their policies stay mirror
images all the way to convergence. Break that symmetry by giving one player a much higher
exploration rate, and the other initially wins more often (it faces random-ish moves), but the
exploring player eventually sees a richer set of boards and catches up.

### Exercise 1.2 — Symmetries

- **Book location:** Section 1.5, PDF page 28.
- **Type:** Theory.
- **Problem:** Many tic-tac-toe positions appear different but are really the same because of
  symmetries. How might we amend the learning process to take advantage? In what ways would this
  change improve the learning process? Now think again: if the opponent did not take advantage of
  symmetries, should we? Is it true that symmetrically equivalent positions should necessarily have
  the same value?
**Hook.** A rotated board is a new string of pixels but not a new situation. Let's see how to make
the learner notice that, and then check whether the opponent's habits can break the trick.

**Formal bridge.** Let \(g\) range over the eight rotations and reflections of the board, and let
\(g s\) be the board \(s\) transformed by \(g\). Learning on equivalence classes \([s] = \{g s :
g\}\) means imposing

\[
v(s) = v(g s) \quad \text{for all } g, \qquad \tilde v([s]) := v(s),
\tag{1.2.1}
\]

with the analogous equivariance condition \(\pi(a \mid s) = \pi(g a \mid g s)\) for the policy.

where \(s\) is a tic-tac-toe board, \(g\) is a symmetry (rotation or reflection) of the board,
\([s]\) is the equivalence class of boards equivalent to \(s\), \(v\) is the state-value function,
\(\tilde v\) is the value defined on classes rather than raw boards, and \(\pi\) is the policy.

**Reasoning chain.** The square board admits eight symmetries — four rotations and four
reflections. Mapping every encountered board to a canonical representative of its class means
that a win learned in one corner counts as evidence for wins in all four corners. The effective
state space shrinks by roughly a factor of eight in the early game, so each class receives many
more visits, and the value estimates become reliable with far fewer games. That is the sample
efficiency gain: not new information, but less duplication of the same information.

Equation (1.2.1) states the constraint we are imposing. For the value, it says the estimate must
be constant on each equivalence class; for the policy, it says that if a move is best in \(s\),
its rotated counterpart is best in \(g s\). When we act, we equip the learner with the same
canonicalisation at test time, so equivalent actions get treated alike rather than needing to be
rediscovered independently.

Now the second question. If the opponent ignores symmetries, does the trick still work? There
are two cases. If the opponent's move distribution is itself symmetric (a uniform random player
is the clearest example), the dynamics are unchanged by \(g\), so (1.2.1) is exactly true and
symmetrising is pure gain. If the opponent has a symmetry-breaking bias — say it always takes the
top-left square when available — then a board and its rotation really do lead to different
opponent behaviour, and their true values need not be equal. In that case merging them introduces
a bias: we are averaging over futures that the opponent does not actually generate. Whether to
symmetrise then becomes a tradeoff between sample efficiency and fidelity, and the agent can
still exploit its own structural knowledge even when the opponent does not.

So the honest answer to "must symmetrically equivalent positions have the same value?" is: they
must when the game dynamics and the opponent's policy are invariant under the symmetry (in
particular when values are defined under optimal play), but not automatically when the
environment itself treats the two boards differently. Symmetry is a property of the whole
agent-environment loop, not of the board drawing alone.

**Why it makes sense.** Picture the extreme of a perfectly symmetric opponent: every rotation of
a board is literally the same probability distribution from the agent's point of view, so
averaging the eight copies is just averaging repeated measurements of one quantity — the variance
drops and the mean is unchanged. Now picture the opposite extreme, an opponent that always plays
top-left: rotating the board rotates the opponent's habit with it only if you rotate the habit too,
which you did not. Averaging then mixes two different worlds, and the direction of the error is
toward the average opponent, washing out exactly the exploitable asymmetry. Symmetrise when the
world is symmetric; keep the distinctions when it is not.

**Check your understanding.** Prediction: replace the uniform random opponent with one biased to
top-left. The merged, symmetry-respecting values become slightly wrong on boards where that bias
matters, while an unsymmetrised learner can win more by exploiting it; the gain from sharing
samples stays, but it now competes with a modelling error.

### Exercise 1.3 — Greedy Play

- **Book location:** Section 1.5, PDF page 28.
- **Type:** Theory / Computation.
- **Problem:** Suppose the reinforcement learning player was greedy — always playing the move that
  brought it to the position it rated best. Might it learn to play better, or worse, than a
  non-greedy player? What problems might occur?
**Hook.** Greedy sounds like the obvious way to use what you have learned — until you ask where
the learning comes from. Let's separate "picking the best move" from "finding out which move is
best".

**Formal bridge.** Greedy selection picks the action with the highest current estimate,

\[
\pi_{\text{greedy}}(s) = \arg\max_a q(s,a),
\tag{1.3.1}
\]

while estimates only improve for actions that are actually sampled, via

\[
q_{k+1}(s,a) = q_k(s,a) + \alpha_k\left[r_{k+1} - q_k(s,a)\right].
\tag{1.3.2}
\]

where \(s\) is the current state, \(a\) is a candidate action, \(q(s,a)\) is the learned action
value, \(\alpha_k\) is the step size at visit \(k\), and \(r_{k+1}\) is the reward observed after
taking \(a\).

**Reasoning chain.** The trouble is the interaction between (1.3.1) and (1.3.2). A greedy player
selects the current winner, so the current winner keeps getting updated while every other action
stays at its initial guess. If that initial guess was wrong — and early estimates are necessarily
rough — the player can lock in a move that merely looked best and never collect the evidence that
would overturn it. In tic-tac-toe this is easy to trigger: a move that looks quiet in the short run
may be the only one that avoids a forced loss two plies later, and a greedy player may never give
it a trial.

The exploratory player breaks the loop. By occasionally choosing a non-greedy move, it generates
samples for those neglected actions, so (1.3.2) can revise their estimates. Over time the estimates
become accurate for all relevant moves, and the greedy policy derived from them genuinely
maximises expected return. Exploration is not a competing objective with greed; it is how the
greedy policy gets something worth being greedy about.

Notice also the two different policies in play: the behaviour policy that gathers data and the
greedy policy we ultimately want to use. Conflating them is the source of the failure. A learner
that is greedy during data collection may indeed look better early on if its first guesses are
lucky, because it avoids visibly bad moves, but it pays later with blind spots. A non-greedy
learner accepts some early losses as the price of information.

**Why it makes sense.** Take the extreme of a single action that is essential but initially rated
worst. A greedy player never tries it and loses; the gap never closes because the estimate never
receives data. Add even a small chance of trying it and eventually the reward arrives, the
estimate rises, and greedy play adopts the winning move. The direction of the effect is therefore
clear: exploration trades a little immediate performance for a large reduction in the risk of a
permanently wrong first impression. If the initial values were already exact, the trade would
reverse — exploration would only cost moves — which shows that the benefit comes from ignorance,
not from randomness itself.

**Check your understanding.** Prediction: introduce a small exploration probability into a greedy
player. Its early win rate dips because it sometimes makes visibly bad moves, but its long-run
win rate rises as previously untried moves get correct values; if we instead initialise every move
at its true value, exploration only hurts.

### Exercise 1.4 — Learning from Exploration

- **Book location:** Section 1.5, PDF page 28.
- **Type:** Theory / Computation.
- **Problem:** Suppose learning updates are applied to moves — including exploratory moves — while
  the step-size parameter is reduced over time (but the exploration tendency is not). How do the state
  values converge differently depending on whether we learn from all moves, or only from the moves
  that were actually taken? Assuming that exploration continues, which set of value estimates is the
  better one, and which would result in more wins?
**Hook.** Suppose we keep exploring forever, but tighten the step size so the estimates settle
down. The question is not *whether* they settle — it is *what* they settle to. The answer is
decided entirely by which moves we update.

**Formal bridge.** If we back up every move we consider, including exploratory ones, the
estimates approach the optimal state values

\[
v_*(s) = \max_{a} \mathbb{E}\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a\right].
\tag{1.4.1}
\]

If we update only the moves the behaviour policy actually executed, they approach instead the
values of that exploratory policy

\[
v_{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s, \pi\right].
\tag{1.4.2}
\]

where \(s\) is the current state, \(a\) is a candidate action, \(G_t\) is the
total discounted return from time \(t\), \(\pi\) is the behaviour policy that keeps exploring,
and the maximum in (1.4.1) is over all actions available in \(s\).

**Reasoning chain.** Notice the only structural difference between
(1.4.1) and (1.4.2): one has a \(\max_a\), the other has an expectation over
\(\pi\). Updating every considered move performs that maximisation implicitly —
each action gets its own backup, and the best one wins as the estimates
converge. Updating only executed moves never asks "what if we had chosen
differently?", so the numbers converge to the return of the policy that did the
choosing, exploration and all.

This matters because exploration does not stop. Under the second scheme the
converged values still describe a wandering policy. Acting greedily from them
then throws away the very exploration that produced them. Under the first
scheme the greedy action is already the one the values were built around, so
the same greedy tie-break keeps paying off. Since the first set of values is
optimal, greedy play from it wins more often in the long run than greedy play
from the diluted values of (1.4.2).

**Why it makes sense.** Think of the extreme: let exploration be uniform and
permanent. A value learned only from executed moves averages over all those
random choices, so it can never exceed the average of the available returns.
A value learned from all moves keeps the best return separately, untouched by
the randomness. The gap between (1.4.2) and (1.4.1) is exactly the gap between
"what a random player gets" and "what a player who picks the best move gets" —
it cannot vanish while exploration continues.

**Check your understanding.** Prediction: shrink \(\epsilon\) toward zero while
keeping the updates on executed moves. The behaviour policy becomes nearly
greedy, so (1.4.2) moves toward (1.4.1) — but the convergence is slower, because
rarely tried actions are now almost never updated. If instead we keep \(\epsilon\)
large and switch to all-move updates, the values stay optimal and only the data
collection is noisy.

### Exercise 1.5 — Other Improvements

- **Book location:** Section 1.5, PDF page 28.
- **Type:** Theory.
- **Problem:** Can you think of other ways to improve the reinforcement learning player? Can you
  think of any better way to solve the tic-tac-toe problem as posed?
**Hook.** Once a learner works at all, "improve it" has several distinct meanings. Let's organise
the options so we can see which one actually buys something for tic-tac-toe.

**Formal bridge.** The target the player is approximating is the Bellman optimality equation for
a known or learned model,

\[
v_*(s) = \max_{a}\sum_{s',r} p(s',r \mid s,a)\left[r + \gamma v_*(s')\right],
\tag{1.5.1}
\]

and every proposed improvement changes one of its ingredients: the data collection policy, the
representation of \(v\), or the use of the model \(p\).

where \(s\) is the current board, \(a\) is a legal move, \(s'\) and \(r\) are the next board and
reward, \(p(s',r \mid s,a)\) is the environment dynamics (the model), \(\gamma\) is the discount
factor, and \(v_*\) is the optimal state value being approximated.

**Reasoning chain.** Starting from (1.5.1), the first axis is exploration. Softmax action selection
or optimistic initial values push the player to sample moves it would otherwise ignore, so the
estimates entering the maximisation are based on broader evidence. The second axis is
representation: a lookup table is fine for tic-tac-toe, but when the board or task grows, a
function approximator can generalise across positions that share features instead of learning
each cell separately. The third axis is the model itself: if \(p\) is known, we can look ahead —
simulate several moves into the future and evaluate leaves with a learned value — which is exactly
what the book's tic-tac-toe player does. A fourth, more task-specific knob is reward and
termination design: making wins, draws and losses explicit and ending episodes cleanly keeps the
target in (1.5.1) well defined.

For tic-tac-toe as posed, the state space is tiny and a model is available, so tabular values plus
lookahead are already a strong combination: the search supplies the "what if" reasoning and the
learned values supply the evaluation of non-terminal leaves. A better solution does not replace
this with something exotic; it combines planning with learning more tightly — for example,
updating values from simulated experience as well as real games, or deepening the search where the
current estimate is uncertain. Improvements should be judged by whether they change the target,
cover it with better data, or compute it more accurately.

**Why it makes sense.** At one extreme, a complete game tree search from the first move needs no
learning at all: the board is small enough to enumerate, so lookahead alone solves the game. At
the other extreme, if the board were much larger, exhaustive search would be impossible and a
table would receive almost no visits per state; then generalisation and learned evaluation are
what make the problem tractable. The book's hybrid sits between these extremes, which is why it
works: it spends computation on a short search and spends samples on learning the values that
end the search.

**Check your understanding.** Prediction: enlarge the game to a \(4\times4\) board with the same
learner. The number of states explodes, so visits per state collapse, tabular estimates get noisy,
and both function approximation and deeper lookahead become much more valuable; if wins are made
rarer or more delayed, optimistic initialisation also starts to matter more.

## What to take away

- The value you converge to is decided by which moves you back up: backing up every move you consider
  gives optimal values with the max built in, while backing up only the moves you executed gives the
  values of the exploratory behaviour policy. Acting greedily on one is not the same as acting
  greedily on the other.
- Self-play reaches a stable policy only when the roles are interchangeable. With mirror-image
  rewards and identical move sets, both learners chase the same minimax target and the stable outcome
  is drawing play; give one player a very different exploration rate and the target moves for both.
- Symmetry belongs to the whole agent–environment loop, not to the board drawing. Canonicalising
  boards multiplies each visit count only if the opponent's behaviour is invariant too; a biased
  opponent turns two rotations into genuinely different futures, so sharing values buys samples at
  the cost of fidelity.
- Greedy action selection and learning pull against each other: the policy keeps choosing the current
  best move, but only sampled moves get revised. A crucial move that starts out looking bad can
  stay bad forever — exploration is how the greedy policy gets something worth being greedy about.
- Watch the thread into later chapters: the split between the policy that gathers data and the policy
  we ultimately want becomes on-policy versus off-policy learning in Chapters 5 and 6, and the
  lookup table here gives way to generalisation once the state space grows.
